# Rise Above: A Workbook for the Adult Who Already Knows the Story
### Companion to *Rise Above* by Derek Lim

You read this as a children's fable once, or you read it to your own children. Underneath the tortoise, the rabbit, and the falcon is a set of patterns that don't disappear once you grow up — they just get quieter, better-dressed, and harder to name.

This workbook names them.

---

## Section 1 — The Overconfidence Pattern
*Ronny's defeat (Chapter 3)*

Ronny didn't lose the first race because he was slow. He lost because he stopped — certain he had already won. The defeat itself wasn't the problem. What mattered was what he did at 2am, replaying it, until he stopped asking *what happened* and started asking *why*.

**Exercise: The Five Whys**

Think of a recent setback — a deal that fell through, a project that stalled, a relationship that cooled. Write it in one sentence. Then ask "why" five times, each answer building on the last, until you reach something that isn't circumstantial.

1. What happened: ___________________________________________
2. Why? ___________________________________________
3. Why? ___________________________________________
4. Why? ___________________________________________
5. Why? ___________________________________________
6. Why? ___________________________________________ ← this is usually the real one.

**Naming it:** Most people stop at the third "why," at the answer that still lets them off the hook. If your fifth answer points at certainty, ease, or a moment you stopped paying attention — that is the overconfidence pattern. It is not a character flaw. It is what happens to anyone who has won enough times to believe the next win is owed to them.

*If this section named something real, it runs through the recurring-difficulty work in* Overcoming Adversity — *specifically the Pattern Repeater profile: the adversity that keeps returning isn't random, it's recurring because its root hasn't been addressed, only its surface.*

## Section 2 — The Identity Threshold
*Tommy's doubt and edge-finding (Chapter 5)*

After his first win, Tommy didn't feel triumphant — he felt hollow, watching Ronny improve and thinking, *He's better than me now. What chance do I have?* That is not false modesty. That is the specific discomfort of standing at the edge of an identity that hasn't caught up to what you're capable of yet.

His answer wasn't to train harder at Ronny's game. It was an honest inventory — and a decision to change the terrain.

Where, right now, are you measuring yourself by a standard that was never built for your strengths — a title, a metric, a comparison to someone whose race isn't yours?

**Naming it:** This is the Identity Threshold — not a lack of capability, but a coherent, functioning sense of self that doesn't yet include the version of you that's about to compete on its own terrain. The discomfort is the threshold, not a verdict.

## Section 3 — From Idea to Working Thing
*Ronny's invention and iteration (Chapters 6–7)*

Ronny didn't build the village's river crossing by having a brilliant idea once. He had a mediocre idea (a log and a rope), tested it, failed publicly, asked the people who'd actually use it what they needed, and rebuilt it — more than once — before it worked.

**Exercise: The Build-Test-Learn Cycle**

Name one vision you've been carrying that hasn't become a real, finished thing.

- What is the vision: ___________________________________________
- What is the *smallest* version of it you could test this week — not the polished version, the log-and-rope version: ___________________________________________
- Who would actually use or benefit from it, and have you asked them what they need — or are you guessing: ___________________________________________

**Naming it:** The gap between a vision and a finished thing is rarely about talent or time. It's about whether you let the first version be rough enough to actually exist.

*Explored fully in* Building Castles in the Sky — *on the internal structure of completion, and the difference between preparation and protection.*

## Section 4 — Water Under the Bridge
*The apology and the team race (Chapter 8)*

Ronny's apology to Tommy wasn't transactional — he didn't apologize to win a rematch. He apologized because the tension had become a cost he was no longer willing to keep paying. What followed — the two of them racing as a team, faster together than either alone — was only possible because the debt was actually settled, not just set aside.

**Exercise: The Unsettled Account**

Name one relationship — professional or personal — where there is still tension that has never actually been addressed, only avoided.

- What actually happened: ___________________________________________
- What you've told yourself instead of addressing it: ___________________________________________
- What an honest, non-transactional apology or conversation would sound like: ___________________________________________

**Naming it:** Most "moving on" is actually just routing around an unsettled account. It works, until you need that person's strength to cover your weakness — and discover the relationship can't bear the weight.

## Section 5 — The Adversity Diagnostic
*Fiona's rejection (Chapter 9) and the day of reckoning (Chapter 10)*

Fiona was rejected — repeatedly, specifically, on the basis of how she looked rather than what she could do — and very nearly left before the race began. What changed wasn't the rejection. It was what she did with it.

This is the same diagnostic question at the center of *Overcoming Adversity*. Answer honestly.

**Which is most true of where you are right now?**

- [ ] **The Pattern Repeater** — I keep solving the same problem and it keeps coming back in a new form.
- [ ] **The Still Point** — I understand exactly what I need to do and still haven't moved.
- [ ] **The Long-Distance Carrier** — I've been resilient for a long time, and I'm closer to the edge of that than I'm admitting.
- [ ] **The Meaning Seeker** — I've stopped being sure any of this is worth what it's costing me.

Whichever you checked, write two or three sentences on what that actually looks like in your week, not in the abstract:

___________________________________________
___________________________________________

**Naming it:** Fiona's breakthrough didn't come from being told to keep trying. It came from two people who'd been there naming, precisely, what was happening to her — *seasons of rejection, not a verdict on her worth* — so she could stop carrying it as identity and start carrying it as weather.

## Section 6 — Stop Running Their Race
*Fiona's solo race (Chapter 11)*

In the team relay, Fiona had teammates and a clear lane. Alone, the old voices came back louder, and for a stretch she ran the others' race — watching the goat, flinching at the crowd — until she remembered what was actually true and rose above the chaos instead of fighting through it.

**Exercise: I Am Built For**

Finish this sentence as precisely as you can, without softening it for an audience:

*I am not too _____________________.*
*I am built for _____________________.*

Now name one place this week where you are currently running someone else's race — competing on a metric, timeline, or comparison that was never built for what you're actually built for.

**Naming it:** Rising above isn't escaping the difficulty. It's gaining enough altitude to see your own course clearly again — the thing Fiona could only do once she stopped trying to win on the ground, in someone else's lane.

## Closing

Six patterns. None of them require you to become someone else. Tommy didn't get faster. Ronny didn't stop being fast — he got focused. Fiona didn't change her wings. Each of them became more precisely themselves under pressure, not less.

If one section here named something you've been circling for a while without quite landing on, that's not a coincidence — it's the same internal work this fable was always pointing toward for the adults reading it to their children.

*Continue the work in* Unleash Your Super Power, Building Castles in the Sky, *and* Overcoming Adversity — *or bring it directly into a coaching conversation.*
